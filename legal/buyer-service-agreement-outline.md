# Buyer engagement letter / service agreement (Q19) — OUTLINE FOR LAWYER REVIEW
_Last checked: 2026-11-09_

> **This is an outline for lawyer review, not a final contract and not legal advice.** The sample wording is illustrative and must be reviewed by a qualified US lawyer before use. Background and sources: `/research/legal-and-contracts.md` (source numbers below refer to that file).

## Summary

- Use a **short engagement letter (2–4 pages)** that a startup founder can sign without their own lawyer. A long contract will slow down or kill small deals.
- Its four jobs: (1) disclose how you are paid, (2) make clear you are **not the provider**, (3) cap your liability, (4) obtain the buyer's compliance statements and your right to refuse.
- **Commission disclosure must be explicit and in writing, and signed or accepted by the buyer.** Undisclosed supplier payments can cost you the fee and can be criminal under state commercial-bribery laws [15][16].
- Choose **one fee model per deal** and name it in the letter. Before choosing a model that includes a buyer-paid fee, check the supplier's partner terms: some remove the commission if the customer pays you [1].
- Never hold the buyer's money for the supplier. The buyer pays the supplier directly.
- Compliance clauses do not transfer your legal duties to the buyer. They support your own screening; they do not replace it.

## Fee models

| Model | How it works | Disclosure needed | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Free sourcing, supplier-paid commission** | Buyer pays you nothing. The chosen supplier pays you a referral commission | That you are paid by suppliers, the typical range, the actual rate on request or in the proposal, and that rates differ between suppliers (a conflict of interest) | Appearing to steer towards the highest commission; no income if the buyer picks a non-partner supplier |
| **B. Transparent buyer fee, no commission** | Buyer pays a fixed fee, retainer, or percentage of first-year contract value. You accept no supplier payment on the deal, or pass any commission to the buyer in full | State that you receive nothing from the supplier, or that anything received is credited to the buyer | Harder to sell to small startups; collection risk |
| **C. Combined, fully disclosed** | Free or low-fee sourcing funded by commission, plus paid extras (benchmark design, negotiation support, procurement management), and/or part of the commission passed to the buyer company as a rebate | Both income streams, in numbers, for the chosen supplier | Conflict with supplier terms that ban customer-paid compensation or "incentives" [1]; more complex to explain |

Rule for every model: **any rebate or pass-back goes to the buyer company, shown on a document, never to an individual at the buyer** [15][16].

| 8 | Limitation of liability | Total liability capped at fees and commissions Broker actually received for the relevant transaction in the prior 12 months (with a small floor amount under Model A); no indirect, consequential, lost-profit or data-loss damages; exceptions for fraud and wilful misconduct | Lawyer to confirm enforceability and required carve-outs in the chosen state |
| 9 | Client compliance representations | See list below | Core clause |

| 13 | Non-exclusivity | Client is free to source elsewhere; Broker may work for other clients, including competitors | |
| 14 | Confidentiality | Mutual; Broker may share the requirement with prospective suppliers (identity only after Client consent); carve-outs for legal and compliance disclosures | |
| 15 | Data protection | Broker handles only business contact and KYC data, as an independent controller; Broker does not access Client workloads, datasets, credentials or accounts | If Broker ever needs account access, a separate agreement is needed |
| 16 | Data-residency and regulatory needs | Client must state residency, sovereignty or sector requirements in the intake form; Broker relays supplier statements but Client must verify them contractually with the supplier | |
| 17 | Term and termination | Until the requirement is fulfilled or either party ends it on short notice (e.g. 14 days); fee and clause 12 obligations survive | |
| 18 | Independent contractor / no fiduciary expansion | Broker is an independent contractor; describe duties as limited to those in the letter | Lawyer to advise how far fiduciary duties can be limited where Broker is paid by the buyer |

| 20 | General | Entire agreement; written changes; e-signature; no assignment without consent | |

## Sample wording for the core clauses (illustrative only)

**Clause 3 — Broker is not the provider**
> Broker is an independent intermediary. Broker does not own, operate, resell or control any computing infrastructure. Any computing services are provided to Client solely by the supplier Client selects, under a contract directly between Client and that supplier. Broker is not a party to that contract, does not act as agent for the supplier, and has no authority to bind the supplier or Client.

**Clause 4 — Commission disclosure (Model A)**
> Broker does not charge Client for the Services. **Broker may receive a referral commission from the supplier Client selects, typically between [X]% and [Y]% of the amounts Client pays that supplier, for [period].** Commission rates differ between suppliers, and some suppliers pay Broker nothing; this is a conflict of interest that Broker manages by presenting all options it considers suitable regardless of commission. Before Client signs with a supplier, Broker will tell Client in writing whether Broker will receive a commission from that supplier and at what rate. By signing, Client consents to Broker receiving such commissions.

**Clause 4 — Model B variant**
> Client will pay Broker [fixed fee / X% of first-year contract value], invoiced [timing]. Broker will not retain any commission or other benefit from a supplier in connection with Client's transaction; anything received will be disclosed and credited to Client.

**Clause 4 — Model C variant**
> In addition to the commission disclosed above, Client will pay Broker the fees in Schedule 1 for the additional services listed there. [Broker will pass [Z]% of any commission received to Client by credit note / payment to Client's company bank account.]

**Clause 7 — No warranty**
> Broker provides the Services with reasonable care but gives no warranty regarding any supplier or its services, including availability, performance, hardware specification, security, uptime, pricing or financial standing. Information from suppliers is passed on as received unless Broker states in writing that it has verified it.

**Clause 9 — Client compliance representations (list)**

Client states, and will promptly tell Broker if any of this changes, that:
1. it has disclosed the true end user and the intended end use of the compute, and is not acting for an undisclosed third party;
2. neither Client, nor its owners or controllers, nor the end user is on a US, EU, UK or UN restricted-party or sanctions list, or located, organised or resident in a comprehensively sanctioned country or territory;
3. it will not use the compute, or allow it to be used, in violation of US export controls or sanctions or other applicable law, including for any prohibited military, weapons or surveillance end use, and will not give access to restricted persons or destinations;
4. it will not use Broker or any supplier to obtain access that it has been refused elsewhere on compliance grounds;
5. payments will come from an account in Client's own name, in a jurisdiction disclosed to Broker;
6. it will comply with the selected supplier's acceptable-use and onboarding requirements;
7. the information given to Broker is complete and accurate.

Plus: Broker may report suspected violations to the supplier and to authorities, and this does not breach confidentiality.

> The exact content of these representations must follow the compliance policy in `/compliance/` and be reviewed by an export-control lawyer. They do not replace Broker's own screening.

## Things to keep out of the agreement

- Any promise of "best price", guaranteed availability, uptime or savings.
- Any statement that you "verified" hardware unless you did, and can show how.
- Holding deposits, escrow, or paying suppliers for the client.
- Access to the client's cloud accounts, keys or data.
- Advice on compute futures or hedging transactions (see Q21 in the research file).
